Every figure here is derived from the monthly prices in the site's own data file, using the rules published on the Strategy page. Where a month is marked ≠, what I actually bought differed from what the rules called for, and both numbers are shown. Figures in US dollars and they include the $3 brokerage my broking app charges on every buy, which is taken from the cash after the rules have set the share count and is separate from anything the fund charges. They ignore FX conversion costs, tax and dividends, so the real return is still a little worse than what you see here. TQQQ's 0.86% management fee is not subtracted here, because a fund charges it inside its own price rather than billing you, so these figures already carry it. Subtracting it again would count it twice. The estimate below shows roughly what it has cost, and there is a fuller explanation on the home page. It cuts the other way too: the cash reserve is credited with interest at 4% a year, accrued monthly, which is roughly what a high-interest saver or offset account pays. That interest counts as return, not as money in, and the figure below shows how much of the gain came from it.

The same money, three funds

What if it had gone into QQQ or QLD instead.

The rules decide how much cash leaves the account each month. This spends that same amount on the two funds below the one I use, at the price each closed at on the day of the buy, and prices the result at today's market. The money going in is the same in all three columns. Only the leverage changes. QQQ tracks the Nasdaq-100 with no leverage, QLD doubles the daily move, TQQQ triples it.

Month Deployed QQQ 1× QLD 2× TQQQ 3×

Each cell is the return to date on everything bought up to that month, with the price that fund closed at on the day of the buy underneath it. Money in is identical across the three columns, so the only thing being measured is the leverage. Four things worth knowing before reading too much into it. All three funds are priced on the same day, the day the order went in, so this is a like-for-like comparison rather than a month-end approximation; where the log does not record a date, the day is inferred as the one whose TQQQ close sits closest to the price in the log, and whichever date was used is printed under the month. The benchmark buys fractional shares, which it has to, because at QQQ's price a month's deployment often would not buy one whole share, and the real portfolio buys whole shares only and strands the rest as cash, so this flatters the benchmarks slightly. Closes are corrected for splits but not for distributions, the same as everywhere else on this site, so QQQ is understated by the most. And the tier ladder is driven by TQQQ's drawdown: a portfolio genuinely built on QQQ would have had its own high-water mark and deployed on different months, so this holds the schedule fixed to isolate the fund choice rather than to claim it is what I would have done.
