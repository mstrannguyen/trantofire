The Strategy

My rules, written down before I needed them.

US$800 a month into a 3× leveraged Nasdaq-100 fund. Sitting close to its record high I invest only a fifth of the cash I'm holding and bank the rest. The further it falls below that high, the bigger the share I spend, and at −60% I spend all of it. It is about a third down as I start, so the first buys are already past the baseline. Every rule is written out below.

The intention

Take more risk when risk is cheap.

Most people do the opposite without meaning to. Confidence rises with the market, so we buy hardest near the top and go quiet after a crash, which is when future returns are best. Counter-cyclical leverage flips that instinct and makes it mechanical, so it doesn't depend on how brave I feel that week.

On the way up my leverage falls. I buy a small slice and let cash pile up next to it. On the way down it rises. The cash converts into shares, faster the deeper it goes. The cash isn't sitting idle. It's waiting for a trigger I already agreed to.

The concept is Henrique Centieiro's counter-cyclical leverage framework, published on TradingView. The drawing is mine; any clumsiness in it is mine too.

Optimal leverage

The point where more leverage stops paying.

Leverage does not just scale a return up. Tony Cooper's 2010 paper works out the long-run compound return of a leveraged fund and finds it traces a hill. Returns climb as leverage rises, reach a peak, then fall away. Go far enough past the peak and the long-run return turns negative while the index underneath it is still going up.

The peak sits at roughly the index's return divided by the square of its volatility. Volatility is squared in that sum and the return is not, so volatility carries far more weight. Everything below follows from that.

The shape of Cooper's result, drawn from his return and volatility figures for the two indexes. The vertical scale is left unlabelled because the height of the hill depends on which decades you measure, so the position of the peak is the only part worth reading off it.

The Nasdaq-100 returned more than the S&P 500 across these windows and it still has the lower ceiling, because it was about a third more volatile and volatility is the term that gets squared. The livelier index is the one that tolerates less leverage. Across the ten markets Cooper tested, the optimum usually landed near 2, reached 3 in a few cases, and never reached 4.

  • Below the peak

    Each extra turn of leverage adds return along with the volatility, so you are being paid for the risk you take on. Being early on the curve also buys you room to be wrong. If the real peak turns out to sit lower than the estimate, you give up part of the gain rather than all of it.

  • Above the peak

    Each extra turn still adds the full volatility and now subtracts return, so you carry more risk to earn less. The error runs the other way too. A stretch of lower returns or higher volatility pulls the peak to the left, and a position that was slightly past it becomes badly past it, with nothing to announce that it happened.

Why I still chose 3×

These peaks are computed across whole market histories, deep bear markets included. Inside a sustained bull run the return term rises and the peak moves right along with it, and that is where a 3× fund earns its keep. It compounds harder than a lower multiple for as long as the trend holds, and I intend to hold through it.

I am also not sitting on a flat 3× position. Cash waits until the market falls, so my exposure runs under three times for as long as the reserve holds anything, and I pay below the average price along the way.

The tier ladder

How much I deploy, by depth.

One number decides everything: how far TQQQ has fallen from its record high. That sets the percentage. The percentage applies to all the cash I have available, which is last month's reserve plus this month's US$800. The price that decides it is the price I actually pay on the day I place the order, not a month-end figure or an average. So the tier is fixed by the same number that appears in my log, and anyone can check the two agree.

Worked example

Four months of a thousand dollars.

Starting from nothing, with US$1,000 going in on the same day every month. The rules take their slice, whatever is left waits for next month, and the market does what it does. This is where the reserve comes from.

The first three months look like nothing is happening. Each one buys a small parcel and leaves four fifths of the cash alone, so by month four there is $1,952 sitting idle.

Then the market falls, and month four puts $1,978 to work in a single buy, nearly ten times what month one managed, at prices 43% below the high. That money only exists because the three months before it refused to spend.

Two things this table leaves out, both of which the real log on the Progress page includes: the reserve earns 4% a year while it waits, and every buy costs $3 in brokerage and gets rounded down to whole shares.

Why not just buy every month

Because of when you start.

The single most important word on this page is every. This only works as a monthly habit held for decades, not as a lump sum dropped in once. A one-off purchase of a 3× fund is a bet on your entry timing. Buying a fixed amount every month, through the good years and the terrible ones, is what turns that bet into a strategy.

Collins makes the point with the worst possible start. Put $1,000 into TQQQ as a single purchase in January 2000, at the top of the dot-com bubble, and twenty years later you are roughly back where you began, because the crash that followed took a simulated 3× Nasdaq down around 99%, and a lump sum has no way to recover from that. Contribute steadily through the same period and the picture changes completely: every crash becomes cheaper shares, and the later recovery lifts all of them. Same fund, same two decades. The difference is entirely whether you kept buying.

Collins tested both against TQQQ, changing only the month you begin. The result depends entirely on where the market was when you started.

Start at the bottom of 2003 and the two finish neck and neck. Nothing is gained by holding cash, because the market only went up from there. Start at the top of 2007, right before the crash, and holding cash finishes about sixteen percent ahead. Same fund, same contributions, same twenty years. The only difference was the starting point, and nobody gets to know theirs in advance.

I am beginning in August 2026 with TQQQ about a quarter below its record high, which is neither a clean low nor a fresh peak. Holding the reserve is what I am paying to not have to guess which one it turns out to be.

There is a second reason, and it is the one I think actually keeps a person in the game. A plain monthly buyer has to endure a crash. Someone holding a reserve gets to use one. On a twenty-year horizon you end up hoping for the pullbacks, because that is when the shares go on sale. That is a completely different experience to watching the number fall with nothing to do about it, and I suspect it is the difference between still being here in year four and quietly giving up.

The discipline this actually requires

None of the numbers on this page happen without one boring behaviour: contributing on schedule, and deploying the reserve when the rules say to, in exactly the months it feels most insane to do so. The 80/20 and tier methods only beat plain buying because they spend into the fear. Skip the contributions during a crash, or freeze and hold the cash "until things calm down," and you have quietly converted the strategy back into a lump-sum bet, the one Collins shows going nowhere for twenty years.

A 2026 paper out of the University of Waterloo gets to the same place from another direction. Forsyth, van Staden and Li test leveraged funds against a century of market data, rebuilt back to 1926 because the funds themselves only date from 2006 and their real record covers an unusually kind stretch of history. They find these funds reward a strategy that decides how much to hold and keeps changing it, and punish one that buys and then sits. Their other warning is about which index sits underneath: a leveraged fund on a broad market behaves nothing like one on a narrow, volatile sector, and most of the horror stories come from the second kind.

So the hard part was never picking the fund. It is being the kind of person who still sends money in during the month the headlines are worst. That is the real experiment here, and I don't know yet whether I can do it.

Three ways to run it

And why I picked the patient one.

Collins ran three approaches over the same nineteen years, all on TQQQ. They are worth seeing side by side, because the "best" one on paper is not the one I chose, and the gap is the whole argument.

Two things jump out. The timing signal had the highest yearly growth and by far the gentlest ride, a 47 percent worst fall against the S&P's 51. On those numbers it is the sensible winner. But it ends with a fraction of the cash-reserve method, because it kept stepping out of the market and missing the biggest up-months, and it only works if I watch a weekly signal and act on it correctly every time for two decades.

The cash-reserve method ends with the most money and asks the least of me. It also has an 86 percent worst fall baked in. That number is not a footnote. It is the price of the ending balance, and it is roughly what I would have to sit through without flinching for any of this to work.

I chose the patient method with my eyes open. Not because it is safest, it plainly is not, but because it is the one I can actually follow without having to be right about timing. My tiers are a steeper version of that middle row: buy least at the top, and a great deal more at the bottom.

Other ways to do this

What I didn't choose, at least not yet.

None of these is a bad idea. They are just not what I am running, and the reasons are below.

  • A lower multiple: QLD

    QLD is the 2× version of the same Nasdaq-100. I went with the 3× because a higher multiple compounds harder through a bull run, and because the cash reserve already does the job a lower multiple would. Holding most of the pile in cash near the highs means my actual exposure is well under 3× most of the time. The leverage only arrives as the market falls, which is when I want it.

  • A different index: UPRO

    UPRO is 3× the S&P 500, broader and less top-heavy than the Nasdaq. Against TQQQ it comes down to risk appetite and which index you want to own. UPRO is the more diversified of the two, and that is a real advantage. But since both funds launched, TQQQ has consistently outperformed it, and that is what decided me.

  • The SMA method

    Collins' second system uses a 14-period moving average to move in and out of the fund, sitting in cash through downturns instead of riding them. It sidesteps the drawdowns that make TQQQ frightening, and on his numbers it works. It also asks me to read a signal every month and to be right about when to get back in. For now I would rather have rules that never ask me to make a call. That is a preference, not a verdict on the method, which on Collins' numbers works well.

  • The 9-Sig strategy

    A separate system, from Jason Kelly rather than Collins. It pairs TQQQ with a bond or cash reserve, starts around 60/40, and every quarter rebalances the TQQQ position back to a 9% growth target, selling the surplus in good quarters and buying the shortfall in bad ones. It shares my instinct exactly: react to price with fixed rules, never forecast. It is on my list, and I may run a separate portfolio on it in future.

  • Timing signals

    No moving averages, no volatility filters, no macro calls. Drawdown from the high is the only input, and anyone can check it against my published figures.

Please read this part twice

How this goes wrong.

A soft website does not make a leveraged strategy safe. I'm publishing the failure modes with the same detail as the rules, because a strategy page that only lists the upside isn't a strategy page. It's a sales pitch.

  • At −60% I have nothing left

    The deepest tier spends the entire reserve. If the fall continues to −70% or −80%, and in 2022 TQQQ fell about 82%, my only ammunition is the next US$800. The rules have no gear below the bottom gear.

  • Daily reset, not 3× the period

    TQQQ targets three times the index's daily move. Over months and years in choppy markets, compounding drags the result well below 3× the index return, sometimes below zero while the index is flat.

  • Currency and concentration

    An Australian buying a US-domiciled fund is also taking an unhedged AUD/USD bet, in an index where a handful of companies carry most of the weight.

  • I have never done this in a crash

    The rules are easy on a spreadsheet. Emptying the entire reserve into a market that has more than halved, in the same month my job might be at risk, is a different thing entirely. That part is untested.

Read the risk section twice. The tier table can wait.

The mechanics

The unglamorous rules.

  • The price I pay is the price that decides the tier

    There is only one price in play each month: whatever TQQQ costs when I place the order. That single number sets the drawdown, the tier, the deployment percentage and the share count, and it is the number published in the log. If I ever get filled at something different from the price I based the decision on, both figures go on the record.

  • Every buy costs $3

    That is what my broking app charges to place the trade, not anything the fund charges. The tier decides the share count on its own, and brokerage comes out of the cash afterwards, so the fee never shrinks the buy, it just leaves a little less in the reserve. Over twenty years that is $720 of brokerage, which the site tracks and reports rather than hiding.

  • Whole shares only

    Multiply the pile by the tier percentage, then round the purchase down to full shares. In a baseline month at $88.09, 20% of an $800 contribution is $160, which buys one share for $88.09. Brokerage of $3 comes out of the cash as well, so $68.91 rejoins the reserve.

  • Fixed in USD, not AUD

    I contribute US$800 a month, so the AUD it costs me changes with the exchange rate. I'm choosing a steady position size in the currency the fund actually trades in, and accepting a variable bill at home.

  • One buy a month

    Same week every month. The tier is read on the day I actually place the order, not the first of the month.

  • Never sell to rebalance

    There is no sell rule at all during accumulation. The tiers only ever control what goes in.
