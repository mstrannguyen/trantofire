The two indexes

Twenty-six years of the S&P 500 and the Nasdaq-100.

Both of my funds are built on an index. SSO follows the S&P 500, about 500 of the largest companies listed in the United States. QLD follows the Nasdaq-100, the hundred largest non-financial companies on the Nasdaq exchange, weighted heavily towards technology. Before doubling either one, here is what each does on its own: $10,000 put in at the end of 1999 and left until the end of 2025, with the four big falls of those years shaded.

From a high to the low that followed The low, with the fall to it
Both charts use the same log scale, so a fall of a given size is the same height wherever it happens. Move across a chart to read any month.

Over those 26 years the two finished close together. $10,000 in the S&P 500 became $73,703, which is 8.0% a year. In the Nasdaq-100 it became $79,597, or 8.3% a year. Each index rose in 20 of the 26 calendar years.

They are close only because of where the count starts. The end of 1999 was three months before the top of the dot-com boom. The Nasdaq-100 then lost 83% and needed until February 2015 to get back to that top. Start the same $10,000 at the end of 2009 instead and by the end of 2025 the S&P 500 has returned about 14% a year and the Nasdaq-100 almost 19%. Most of what is said about the Nasdaq-100 today comes from those later years.

Both indexes went through all four. The S&P 500 was cut by more than half once, in the financial crisis. The Nasdaq-100 was cut by more than half twice, and it also fell more than 20% in late 2018 and in early 2025, recovering inside a year both times. The speed varies as much as the depth. In 2020 the S&P 500 lost a third in 33 days and had it back in under six months. The financial crisis took it 17 months to reach its low and almost five years to get back.

A fund that moves twice as far as its index every day goes through each of these falls and goes further. SSO and QLD both began in June 2006, so they have been through three of the four. In the financial crisis SSO lost about 85% and QLD 84%. In 2020 they lost 59% and 52% in about a month. In 2022 they lost 47% and 64%. Neither existed in 2000, and doubling the Nasdaq-100's daily moves through that fall works out to a loss of more than 98%. These are the two funds I buy every month.

The family of funds

What these things actually are.

What is inside them

Each of these funds owns the shares in its index, the same companies a plain index fund owns. QLD holds every company in the Nasdaq-100, and on 20 November 2026 the largest was Apple, at 7.6% of the fund. SSO holds all of the S&P 500's companies. A plain fund stops there, with all of its money in the shares.

A leveraged fund puts most of its money into those shares and then adds contracts on the same index, until each dollar in the fund moves like two dollars, or three. Most of that top layer is swaps with about ten investment banks, and the rest is index futures. About two thirds of SSO and about four fifths of QLD is the shares themselves. The contracts on top are what make the funds move twice as far.

The money that is not in shares sits in Treasury bills and a money market fund. That is how a fund the size of TQQQ can promise three times a daily move without owning three times the stock.

Where that fee actually goes

Nobody sends you a bill for it. An ETF takes its management fee out of its own assets, a little every day, which lowers the fund's net asset value. The market price follows that value. So QLD's 0.89% is not charged to you on top of the price, it is charged inside it.

Put plainly: when a fund shows $66.00, that $66.00 is already after the fee. If the fund had charged nothing, the price would have been slightly higher.

That is why this site never subtracts the management fee from the portfolio value. Doing so would count it twice, and the number on the Progress page would stop matching a broker statement. Instead the site estimates what the fee has cost and reports it as a separate line, so the drag is visible rather than buried. Brokerage is the opposite case, and a different thing entirely. The $3 is what my broking app charges to place the trade. It has nothing to do with ProShares or the fund. It leaves my own cash account on every buy, so it is deducted for real.

The pattern is simple. Four versions of the same idea: pick an index, pick a multiple. Nasdaq-100 or S&P 500, doubled or tripled. I want to sit at about twice the index and stay there with as little maintenance as possible, so I buy the two doubles: QLD on the Nasdaq-100 and SSO on the S&P 500. The tier rules decide how fast the money goes in.

One thing worth clearing up

Holding a leveraged ETF is not the same as borrowing money to invest. When you buy on margin, you owe a debt, you pay interest on it, and if the market falls far enough your broker can force a sale to recover the loan. That is a margin call, and it is how leveraged individuals get wiped out at the worst possible moment.

A leveraged ETF works differently. The leverage lives inside the fund, built with swaps and futures on top of the shares it owns, and the fund manages it. You buy a share like any other share. The most you can ever lose is what you put in. There is no loan in your name, no interest bill arriving each month, and no margin call can come for you. If the fund falls 90%, your holding falls 90% and that is the end of it. Painful, but bounded.

That is the one genuine mercy of these funds, and it is why a strategy like mine is even thinkable. I can hold through an 80% drawdown without anyone forcing me out. A margin trader attempting the same thing would very likely be sold out near the bottom, which is the exact opposite of what the rules here tell me to do.

How it works

Dollar-cost averaging, with a plan for the dips.

Two ideas doing one job. The first is deliberately dull. The second is what turns a falling market from something to dread into a schedule of larger purchases.

Every month, no exceptions

Dollar-cost averaging

A fixed US$800 goes into each fund every month, whatever the price is doing. It takes the decision I'm worst at, picking the right moment, off the table completely. Part of it buys shares now. The rest waits as cash.

Only as the market falls

Buying the dips

How much of that cash I deploy is set by one number: how far the fund sits below its high. Near the top I spend a little and let the reserve grow. The deeper the fall, the more I commit.

Money that went in once at the end of 2021 waited 29 months to get back to level. US$10,000 was US$3,936 a year later and US$10,011 at the end of May 2024. The monthly buyer put in US$23,200 over those months and held shares worth US$37,285, which is 61% more than they paid. Every purchase after the first was made below the starting price. The US$800 of January 2023 bought 45.7 shares, where the first US$800 had bought 18.0. Their average price came to US$27.73 a share, in a fund that ended at US$44.56.

The ladder put in the same US$23,200 and changed the size of each purchase. It spent US$160 of the first US$800 and kept the rest. As QLD fell through each rung it spent a larger share of the cash it held, and twice, in October 2022 and January 2023, it spent all of it. Its average price was US$26.17 a share. It finished with US$35,258 in shares and US$2,491 in reserve, US$464 more than the monthly buyer.

That is a small gain, and it comes from starting at the top, which is the case the reserve is for. The reserve is never large. Near the high, at a fifth of the cash each month, it levels off at about four months of contributions. When the fund climbs instead, that waiting cash is a cost. Start both again at the end of 2022 and run them to the end of 2025, and each puts in US$28,800. The monthly buyer finishes with US$52,544 and the ladder with US$50,049, which is 4.7% less. In both periods most of the result came from buying every month. Why I hold a reserve anyway is on the Strategy page.

The engine

Three times the day, not three times the decade.

A leveraged fund aims for a multiple of its index's move on a single day, then resets and does it again tomorrow. TQQQ targets three times the Nasdaq-100, QLD targets two. Because those daily results compound on one another, a long climb carries the fund far past a plain multiple of the index. QQQ, the plain tracker, turned $10,000 into about $129,000. Tripling that return would have reached roughly $366,000. The 2× fund on the same index reached about $565,000. TQQQ actually reached about $1.44 million. That is not three times QQQ. It is about eleven times it, and about four times what tripling the index implies. The daily reset, compounding day after day through one of the strongest runs the Nasdaq has ever had, is the whole reason. (This is a lump sum left untouched, to show the fund's own behaviour. My actual plan buys a fixed amount every month instead, which behaves quite differently. That is what the Progress page tracks.)

It cuts both ways, and hard. Look at 2022: the index fell 33% and TQQQ fell 79%, wiping out four fifths of a balance in a single year. The same compounding that built the tall line hollows the fund out in any market that chops sideways or falls. That is the risk this whole plan is built around, not a footnote to it. What a fall far worse than that did to one purchase made at the top in 2000, and to someone who kept buying every month, is on the Strategy page.
